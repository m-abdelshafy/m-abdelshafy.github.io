### **The Investment Gap and Growing Demand**

Africa, home to almost a fifth of the world's population, receives less than 3% of global energy investment. This figure starkly contrasts with the continent's needs, as it hosts the vast majority (around 83-86%) of the global population lacking electricity access – estimated at approximately 570-600 million people (1). While Africa's total primary energy consumption has historically lagged, this indicates significant potential for development. Indeed, Africa’s average annual energy consumption growth rate has outpaced the global average in recent decades (3). According to the IEA’s 2022 energy outlook, Africa’s energy demand was projected to increase by a third between 2021 and 2030 from its 2021 baseline (4).

Bridging this energy gap requires substantial investment. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates that Africa needs at least USD 22 billion annually to achieve universal electricity access by 2030, plus an additional USD 4 billion annually for clean cooking access (2). Estimates for broader infrastructure needs range from USD 130-170 billion per year (5). To ensure this development aligns with climate goals and allows the continent to leapfrog polluting fossil fuels, the majority of this investment must target renewable energy sources. However, despite recent growth in clean energy funding, fossil fuels still accounted for a significant portion of energy investment in Africa as of 2022/2023 (2, 13).

There are grounds for optimism. Investment in renewable energy (RE) in Africa reportedly more than doubled in 2023, reaching a record USD 15 billion and adding nearly 8 GW of renewable capacity (6). However, even this accelerated trajectory falls short of the African Union's ambitious target of 300 GW of installed renewable capacity by 2030 (6). This analysis examines the drivers behind the recent investment increase, identifies potentially persistent trends, and suggests policy actions to enhance Africa's attractiveness for RE investment amid shifting funding landscapes.

### **The Shifting Funding Landscape**

Global investment in the energy transition surpassed USD 2 trillion for the first time in 2024, although the growth rate slowed to 11% compared to the 24-29% rates seen in 2021-23 (7). Africa's share remained low, estimated at around 2.3% of global renewable energy investment in 2023 (6).

Analysis of the period 2012-2021 by the Carnegie Endowment reveals that energy finance to Africa from G20 countries and Multilateral Development Banks (MDBs) totaled approximately USD 346 billion, averaging USD 35 billion per year. While this average aligns with the lower end of estimated annual needs (USD 31.5-45 billion), its distribution was highly uneven. Over this period, gas/LNG projects received the largest share (24%), followed by mixed fossil fuel projects (17%). Solar (11%) and wind (4%) combined received less funding than gas/LNG alone, though public finance for coal and oil projects saw significant declines after 2018/2019 (13).

Two notable *renewable energy* trends emerged in 2023 that are likely to shape the coming years:

1. **Dominance of Small-Scale Solar:** Investment in small-scale solar reportedly overtook utility-scale solar investment in Africa for the first time in 2023, attracting 65% of solar investment (8). This surge was significantly driven by deployment in South Africa (spurred by load-shedding and policy incentives) and growth in markets like Nigeria and Morocco (6). This trend is expected to persist, fueled by the expansion of Pay-As-You-Go (PAYGo) financing models and the growth in Productive Use of Renewable Energy (PURE) applications, such as solar refrigeration and irrigation.

2. **Resurgence of Geothermal Investment:** After a period of low activity, investment in geothermal energy saw a significant boost in 2023, primarily driven by projects reaching financial close in Kenya (6). Africa's Great Rift Valley holds substantial geothermal potential (estimated at up to 30 GW), offering reliable renewable energy crucial for improving energy access and security in East Africa.

### **Sources of Finance:**** Concentration and DFI Challenges**

The source of energy finance for Africa is highly concentrated. During 2012-2021, public sources (G20 governments, MDBs) provided 57% (USD 197 billion) of the total tracked finance, while private sources (primarily FDI) accounted for 43% (USD 149 billion) (13). However, the vast majority (82%) of this funding originated from just ten financiers (including China, the World Bank Group, Italy, France, and the US). Similarly, funding was heavily concentrated geographically, with ten African countries (Egypt, Mozambique, Nigeria, South Africa, Angola, Morocco, Ghana, Uganda, Kenya, Ethiopia) receiving 77% of the total finance. This concentration risks leaving many nations with significant energy needs underfunded.

While multilateral and bilateral DFIs remain crucial, providing the bulk of recent climate finance (9), over-reliance on them presents challenges:

- **Project Size Bias:** DFIs often favor larger, centralized projects, potentially overlooking smaller, distributed solutions that can rapidly scale access.

- **Market Development Issues:** Concessional DFI loans can crowd out local financial institutions and private investors, hindering the development of domestic capital markets.

- **Risk Aversion:** DFIs may avoid investments in politically unstable regions or challenging segments like informal settlements and smallholder agriculture.

- **Currency Risk:** The prevalence of foreign currency loans exposes borrowers to exchange rate volatility and can increase energy costs.

Moving beyond the traditional DFI "originate-to-hold" model is essential. An "originate-to-distribute" or "originate-and-syndicate" model, where DFIs initiate projects and then sell or co-finance them with institutional investors (pension funds, insurance companies) once de-risked, could unlock capital faster, mobilize more private finance, foster an African green debt market, and increase deal velocity ([cite: User analysis based on DFI challenges]). Public finance, which primarily utilized loans (77% over 2012-2021), followed by guarantees/insurance (19%), could more strategically deploy guarantees and equity to crowd-in private capital, especially for renewables where private finance showed proportionally higher investment than public finance during that period (13).

### **China's Evolving Role in African Energy Finance**

China has historically been a major financier of Africa's energy sector, particularly through large loans from its policy banks (CDB and CHEXIM) for infrastructure projects, with a significant focus on hydropower and fossil fuels (2). However, this landscape is shifting. Following a peak around 2016, lending from these policy banks slowed significantly, and they reportedly issued no new energy loan commitments to Africa in 2022 (10). Concurrently, Chinese commercial banks and state-owned enterprises acting as suppliers gained prominence (11). In 2021, China pledged to stop funding new overseas coal power projects and increase support for green energy (10). This policy shift appears reflected in 2023 data, which showed Chinese DFI lending resuming, albeit at a much smaller scale (around USD 502 million), and focusing exclusively on renewable energy projects (solar, hydro, transmission) in a limited number of African countries (10). While hydropower was a cornerstone of past Chinese investment, the recent, smaller-scale commitments suggest a potential pivot towards a greener, possibly more diversified portfolio, aligning with the "small is beautiful" approach discussed in recent policy forums (10).

### **Impact of Power Africa's Potential End**

The United States has been a significant source of public energy funding for Africa, with its Power Africa initiative serving as a key coordinating platform (13). Launched with ambitious goals to add 30 GW of capacity and 60 million new connections (14), Power Africa's direct budget allocation since 2013 totaled USD 1.2 billion by early 2025. This funding reportedly helped catalyze USD 29 billion in financing from other public and private sources. While assessments of its overall effectiveness vary, the initiative provided a structure that facilitated private sector engagement, particularly from US companies (15). The potential discontinuation or fragmentation of Power Africa could hinder US actors' ability to navigate African energy markets effectively and might reduce the mobilization of private capital. This potential vacuum underscores the need for enhanced coordination among African nations and institutions to maintain investment momentum, develop shared knowledge resources, and improve the continent's energy market attractiveness for diverse international capital.

### **Policy Recommendations: Learning from Success**

Several African nations have implemented successful policies driving renewable energy investment, offering valuable lessons:

- **Kenya:** Government-led exploration significantly de-risked geothermal development, leading to substantial capacity growth.

- **South Africa:** The Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme (REIPPPP) utilized competitive bidding to attract billions in private investment for wind and solar projects.

- **Morocco:** Established the dedicated Moroccan Agency for Sustainable Energy (MASEN) to implement an integrated national strategy, pioneering innovative solar thermal (CSP) and wind projects, often incorporating storage solutions.

- **Egypt:** Employed feed-in tariffs (FiTs) and long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) to attract significant international investment, particularly in large-scale solar projects.

- **Nigeria:** Focused on decentralized solutions by establishing mini-grid regulations and the Nigeria Electrification Project (NEP), aiming to expand rural energy access and create local jobs.

- **Ethiopia:** Launched the Scaling-Up Renewable Energy Program (SREP) with international support, targeting the development of its geothermal and wind resources.

### **Conclusion**

Africa stands at a critical juncture, possessing immense renewable energy potential alongside a profound energy access deficit. While recent years have witnessed encouraging growth in renewable energy investment, particularly in small-scale solar and geothermal, the overall scale remains far below what is needed to meet the continent's development goals and climate commitments. The high concentration of funding from a few international sources and into a handful of recipient countries highlights a significant challenge. Overcoming the limitations of traditional DFI financing models, navigating the evolving roles of major partners like China and the US, and strengthening domestic resource mobilization are crucial steps. The diverse policy successes across the continent—from Kenya's geothermal strategy to South Africa's REIPPPP and Nigeria's focus on mini-grids—demonstrate that tailored, well-implemented national strategies can unlock significant investment. Ultimately, achieving a sustainable energy transformation for Africa requires a concerted effort to blend international support with local ownership, foster innovation in financing, address the distributional inequities in funding, and replicate proven policy models to attract the scale and type of capital needed for inclusive and resilient growth across the entire continent.
